## Decision

**Atropos.** The Fate who cuts the thread — her sisters spin and measure it,
she holds the shears. The meaning maps onto the wedge without explanation:
not another inventory of what exists, but the one cut that collapses the most
paths.

Chosen after a 70-domain sweep established that the whole classical register —
Gordian, Ariadne, Theseus, Nodus, Groma, Dioptra, Occam, Tropos, Limen, Pons,
Cardo — and the whole celestial register — Mintaka, Alnitak, Alnilam, Lodestar,
Merak — are registered across `.ai` `.io` `.com` `.co` `.dev`. Most are parked
rather than used, so the register is purchasable, not closed.

Open items carried forward, neither blocking the build:

- **Domain.** `atropos.*` is parked across TLDs. Broker approach required;
  expect low-to-mid four figures.
- **Trademark.** Atropos Health (Stanford spinout, real-world clinical
  evidence) exists in a different class. Worth a formal opinion before the
  name goes on anything public.

Rejected on collision grounds, recorded so they don't get re-proposed:
**Horizon** (Horizon3.ai — $100M raised, ~6,400 customers, positioned as
"prove what an attacker could reach", plus five more security entities on the
word) and **Orion** (Orion Security — $32M Series A, Feb 2026, IBM
participating, AI-driven DLP; and SolarWinds Orion, which owns that phrase in
every CISO's memory).

Names verified available during the sweep, if the domain acquisition stalls
and a fallback is needed: `cutpath.ai`, `tarjan.ai`, `mincut.ai`,
`hopcount.ai`, `bivium.ai`, plus `cutset.co` / `.dev` / `.sh`.

The mark survives the rename unchanged: three dots on a diagonal, one gold
`#E0A526` stroke crossing them at the point they align. Path, then cut.
*Atropos* says in Greek what the mark says in geometry.

### Top three, argued

**1. Cutset — recommended.**
The only candidate where the name *is* the product's core computation. It needs
zero explanation and gets *better* the more you explain it: min-cut in graph
theory, minimal cut set in fault-tree analysis — so both the security graph
people and the safety/reliability people land on it instantly. No security-market
collision. Trivially sayable and spellable on a call.

It names the second half of the pitch — the fix, not the seeing. That's correct.
The seeing is table stakes and will be commoditized (see Part 2). The cut is the
wedge, so the wedge is what should be on the door.

Cost: `.com` and `.ai` are both held. You're on `getcutset.com` day one and
negotiating for the real one, or you take `cutset.co`. That's a real but small
credibility tax.

Minor tension worth naming: "cut" implies destruction, and Tier 3's promise is
*reversible*. Defensible — you cut edges in a graph, not systems — but expect the
question in a security review.

**2. Tarjan.**
`tarjan.ai` is available clean right now, which is genuinely rare. The meaning is
an insider handshake: anyone who has implemented choke-point analysis knows
Tarjan's algorithm is exactly how you find articulation points and bridges. To
everyone else it's an ownable surname, which is how Splunk, Palantir, and Wiz all
work.

Two costs: it's a living person's name, and on a bad phone line "Tarjan" is
"Tarzan." That second one is a real sayability hit for a name whose whole job is
being said on calls.

**3. Throughline.**
The most self-explanatory of the set — "the throughline from a public Jira
comment to your customer table" is a sentence you'd say in a demo anyway. Longer
than ideal for a logo, and it carries consumer/media baggage (NPR podcast, a
media-training firm), though the security market is clear.

## Part 2 — The challenge pass

### 1. The weakest assumption

**That the agent fabric is a distinct graph worth its own product.**

Every edge in the model — permission, reachability, credential inheritance — is
an IAM edge. An agent is a workload with an identity. An MCP server is a service
with an identity. "Untrusted input → agent → tool → data store" is, structurally,
an over-permissioned non-human identity path, and CIEM/ITDR vendors have been
graphing exactly that for five years. On this reading we are not adding a new
graph. We are adding new *node labels* to an existing one — and a label taxonomy
is a feature, not a company. Which is precisely why Wiz can ship it in a quarter
by joining their cloud graph to an agent-registry parser.

Our best answer is that the untrusted-input node is genuinely new — prompt
injection has no analogue in the IAM graph. That's true. Here's the return volley,
and it's the one that actually lands:

> Prompt injection is a probabilistic, content-dependent edge, and you are
> modeling it as a static config edge. You can tell me an agent *can* read a
> public Jira and *can* call a tool. You cannot tell me an injection will
> traverse it — that depends on the model, the system prompt, the guardrails,
> and the phrasing. So the one edge that makes your graph new is the one edge
> you can't compute from config. Your graph is therefore either (a) the old IAM
> graph with new labels, or (b) speculative on its most important edge. Pick one.

This is the same critique attack-path vendors ate for years — *theoretically
possible, not exploitable* — and XM Cyber's answer was simulation. We have no
equivalent for the injection edge, and agentless means we can't build one the
same way.

There is a good answer, but it costs something. Don't claim the path is
*exploitable* — claim it is *unauthorized by design*. We're not predicting an
injection; we're showing that this agent's trust boundary is wrong whether or not
anyone exploits it. That is fully defensible from config.

But it changes the product from pentest to architecture governance, which is a
different buyer, a different budget, and a slower sale. **Right now the demo
implies exploitability and the data only supports policy.** Pick the story
deliberately, because the gap between them is where a POC dies.

### 2. What kills us — assume Wiz or Palo Alto ships agent path analysis in 9 months

They will. It's a data-source project for them, not a research project: they
already have the graph, the connectors, the tenant model, and the query language.
Assume 9–12 months, "good enough," and bundled free.

Honestly:

- **The graph does not survive.** Neither does the AI-BOM — that was already gone.
- **The choke-point algorithm does not survive.** Algorithms don't hold.
- **Two things survive.**

  *The remediation execution layer.* Platforms are structurally slow to take write
  access into customer environments. For a public company with a large installed
  base, Tier 2/3 is a multi-year internal fight with legal and risk. That gap is
  real and it's measured in years, not quarters.

  *The non-cloud half of the fabric.* Wiz's graph is strong exactly where
  AWS/Azure/GCP are, and weak exactly where the agent fabric will actually live:
  SaaS agents, Copilot/Agentforce/Glean, MCP servers on laptops and in CI,
  Slack/Teams bots. If our graph is only as good as cloud config, we are inside
  their kill zone. If it is strongest where their connectors aren't, we're not.

So: **if the company is "path analysis for agents," nothing survives. Say it
plainly — nothing.** If the company is "the remediation control plane for agent
identity," Wiz shipping path analysis makes us *more* valuable: they generate
findings, someone has to close them, and they will integrate us before they build
us.

Which surfaces a contradiction in the current plan. The architecture section says
remediation *is half the product*. The build order puts it at position **6**. We
are sequencing our only defensible asset last, behind five things that get
commoditized. That's backwards.

### 3. Is agentless the right constraint?

Agentless is right for acquisition and wrong for proof — but the fix is not a
sensor, and I think the constraint has been drawn in the wrong place.

The problem with config-only: it tells you *capability*, not *usage*. "This agent
CAN read the CRM and CAN post externally" lands in a room that has spent five
years learning to discount "can." The credibility gap gets filled by whoever can
say *"and on Tuesday it did."*

But the agent fabric is unusually well-instrumented by default, because
everything goes through an API you can read after the fact:

- Model-provider audit logs (Anthropic/OpenAI enterprise admin APIs; Bedrock,
  Vertex, Azure OpenAI invocation logs — most already landing in
  CloudTrail/Azure Monitor)
- MCP server and gateway logs; LangSmith/Langfuse traces where they exist
- Entra sign-in and OAuth grant/consent logs — actual token usage per NHI
- CloudTrail for what the agent's role actually called
- GitHub audit log for what the agent actually did in repos

All read-only API. No sensor, no deployment, 15-minute onboarding intact. **Fully
inside the agentless constraint.**

So the real question was never agentless vs. agent. It's **capability-only vs.
capability + observed usage** — and we can have observed usage agentlessly. If we
build from static config alone, we're trading away the runtime signal for nothing.

Concrete change: add a fourth edge property alongside permission, reachability,
and credential inheritance — **`observed`**. Every edge is either `possible` or
`possible + exercised (n times, last seen)`.

That one change does three things:

1. Findings become credible. "Possible and exercised 400 times last month" is a
   different conversation than "possible."
2. It gives us the choke-point ranking function that actually matters — cut the
   edges that are *used*, or prove an edge is dead before revoking it.
3. **It makes Tier 3 buildable at all.** "Revoke unused OAuth grant," "expire
   stale NHI token," "disable orphaned agent" — every one of those hinges on the
   word *unused/stale/orphaned*, and none of them are computable from config. You
   can only prove disuse from 90 days of logs. **As specified, Tier 3 cannot be
   built.** This is the thing to fix first.

### 4. A sharper wedge

Same graph, same connectors, different buyer:

> **Sell to the team building the agents, not the team defending against them.**
> The buyer is the Head of AI Platform who has been told by their CISO that
> production is blocked until they can explain what their agents can reach. The
> product is the artifact that gets the agent *approved* — a trust-boundary map,
> a diff that narrows scope, and a signed record of what changed. Same graph,
> same connectors, inverted framing: not a cost center that finds problems, but
> the thing that ships the AI initiative on time. The budget lives inside the AI
> program, which is the only line growing without a ceiling right now, and the
> sale doesn't require the buyer to already believe agents are a threat — which
> at budget level, most CISOs still don't.

Why it may beat the current plan: it removes the two hardest objections at once —
"is this exploitable?" (an approver doesn't need exploitability, they need
completeness) and "who has budget for this?" It also puts us upstream of Wiz,
where being pre-deployment is a moat that a posture platform can't easily reach.

Why it might not: builders churn harder than CISOs, the ACVs start smaller, and
you personally have twenty years of CISO relationships and zero of AI-platform-lead
relationships. That's a real asset to walk away from.

Runner-up wedge, worth one call to test: **agent access review as audit
evidence.** EU AI Act and internal audit are going to demand "prove which agents
can touch regulated data and show me the review." Compulsory recurring budget, no
competition from Wiz, immune to the exploitability objection. Weaker company if
agents boom, but a strong bridge if we're early.

### 5. Kill criterion

**Walk away if enterprises can't produce graph density.**

Path math only beats a spreadsheet at scale. Choke-point analysis on a 40-node
graph is a slide, not a product — the value appears at hundreds of nodes with
non-obvious transitive reachability. If the real graph is small, we're selling an
inventory list with extra steps, and that market is already lost.

Evidence in the first ten calls that triggers it:

- **They can list their production agents from memory.** Fatal on both axes — no
  discovery value and no density.
- **Every agent is read-only or chat-only, no tool with write access.** No path
  terminates anywhere that matters, so v1 detection returns empty on real tenants.
- **Nobody can name the owner of an agent.** This sounds like it validates us. It
  doesn't — it means there's nobody to sell remediation to and nobody to accept
  the Tier 1 PR.
- **"What would you do with this finding?" → "I'd ask the team that built it to
  look at it."** No remediation surface. Tier 1 PRs go nowhere.
- **They already consider agents covered by existing IAM review, and can
  articulate why.** That's assumption #1 confirmed against us.

Positive counter-signal to hunt for: someone who has already had an incident or
near-miss and **built a spreadsheet** to track agent permissions. The spreadsheet
is the proof of demand — it means the pain is real and the current tooling
doesn't cover it.

Honest read on timing: we may be 12–18 months early on density and exactly on
time on mindshare. That's a fundable gap, but it means the first 18 months are
design partners rather than revenue — which argues for the builder wedge as a
bridge, not just as an alternative.

## Part 3 — What I'd change

1. **Reorder the build.** Remediation moves from position 6 to position 2–3. It's
   the only piece that survives a platform vendor entering, and it's the piece
   they'll integrate rather than rebuild.
2. **Add `observed` as a first-class edge property**, sourced agentlessly from
   audit logs. Tier 3 is not buildable without it, and findings aren't credible
   without it. Agentless is preserved.
3. **Decide the story explicitly** — exploitability or architecture governance.
   The demo currently implies the first; the data supports the second.
4. **Name: Cutset.** Buy the fallback domains tonight, broker the real ones.
5. **Ten calls before any code**, structured to test node density — "how many
   agents do you have with write access to something that matters, and who owns
   them" — not to test interest in the idea. Interest is cheap and will be high.
